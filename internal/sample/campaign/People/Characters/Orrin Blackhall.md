# Orrin Blackhall

*Human, 34. Sworn to the Compact in the old words, at twenty-one, in front of
witnesses who are now dead or estranged.*

## At a glance

Orrin is a large, quiet, unbothered man who has spent nine years in a state that
colleagues describe as *settled* and his wife describes as *away*. He works. He
drills the fen-watch recruits at Greywake in the mornings and sits at the Kettle
in the evenings and does not drink, which in Greywake is a statement.

He is also the only living person who swore the Compact in person, in the old
words, with the full formula. He does not know whether that binds him any more.
He has not tested it, because testing it would require doing something he has
sworn not to do, and he suspects that is exactly the mechanism.

## What he wants

He tells the table he wants the letter delivered, because that is the job. What
he wants is to be *released* — from the oath, from Greywake, from the version of
himself that a nineteen-year-old made in one afternoon in a cold room.

He is not sure the Assembly is wrong. He has read their case. He thinks they are
right about the binding and wrong about what follows from unbinding, and he has
no plan for telling them that without sounding like a man making an argument for
his own life.

## How to play him

- Orrin takes the rear guard position every single time and nobody has to ask.
- He is the one who says the thing nobody wants to say, *once*, and then never
  brings it up again. He does not relitigate.
- He is honest to the point of rudeness and has no idea this is a trait.
- Under a charm or a compulsion he gets *more* honest, not less, and this will
  be genuinely dangerous in session four.

## Notes for the game master

Orrin is the campaign's conscience and its worst decision. Every scene he is in
should offer him exactly one good reason to break the oath and one good reason
not to, and the table should be able to argue both without the game master
arguing either.

He is also the natural bearer of a truth the party needs, and the most expensive
possible bearer, because he will only say it if he is certain it is true, and he
has not been certain of anything since the breaking.

> [!tip] Use the assembly scene
> The Quiet Assembly's offer in session three is written for him specifically.
> It is the only offer in the campaign that costs Orrin nothing, and it is the
> one he will refuse, and the refusal is what starts session four.

## His oath, in the words he swore

The old words, as Orrin swore them, in the room over the chandler's shop on Salt
Street with four witnesses present:

> *"I give this oath as my own and not as my house's, and I give it in the ninth
> hour, and I hold it until the Compact is discharged in full or I am discharged
> by the Compact itself, and if neither then until I am dead, and being dead does
> not discharge it."*

That last clause was not in the printed form. Orrin noticed at the time. He has
never told anyone he noticed. He can recite the whole thing, and he has never
once performed it, and if a character asks him to, he changes the subject in a
way that is obviously a change of subject.

### The two answers, and why both of them are true

1. **The binding was real.** The Compact's words held, and the crown was the
   mechanism, and the breaking severed the *obligation of every city at once* —
   which is why eight cities went silent within a fortnight and not within a
   year.
2. **The binding did not release the individual.** Orrin's oath named *him*, and
   what the crown did was release the cities. He is not free. He has been the
   only sworn man in Greywake for nine years and there was never a second one,
   because nobody else swore personally after the breaking.

If both hold, Orrin's tragedy is that being released was never on the table. He
has to *discharge* the Compact, and discharging it is an act, and the act is
what the campaign is.

> [!danger] Do not resolve either answer before session four
> The table should be able to argue both of these from evidence it has gathered.
> Revealing either one removes the choice. The commonest early mistake is a
> player reasoning out answer two in session two and being right, and the game
> master having to either confirm it or lie. Let it sit.
